The sofas in the Learning Commons (LYH 208)—they are truly not meant for human rest. Believe me, this is a 'hard-learned' lesson from someone who has been there. It was late at night before an exam, and when I finally finished studying the last chapter and looked at my phone, I was shocked to see it was already 2:00 AM. At that moment, curled up on that rock-hard sofa, I felt no sense of satisfaction. What we call 'pulling an all-nighter' is often just a final, desperate struggle resulting from poor time management.

University offers students unprecedented freedom, but the price of that freedom is the responsibility to manage your own time. If you don't want to spend your exam season sleeping on the hard chairs of the library, please be sure to learn the following principles of time management.

### Define Your 'Priorities'

You must determine the **order of priority** among your academic research, extracurricular organizations, career exploration, and personal relationships. Academics are only one part of university life.
That semester, I received an invitation to a banking industry event that offered direct insight into industry trends and culture. The cost was missing two days of classes. I thought to myself, 'It’s only two days of progress; it shouldn't be hard to catch up.' The result was that I ended up having to spend even more time catching up later.
You need to learn to assess: Do I have more important things to do? A clear goal can help you filter out ineffective social commitments and activities, allowing you to invest your energy in the most critical tasks.

Goals also need to be **time-bound and measurable**. You might try inputting all your assignment deadlines and exam dates into your calendar during the first week of the semester, allowing the pressure of 'deadlines' to transform into the motivation to take action rather than procrastinate.

### Create Time for 'Deep Work'

You must carve out time for 'deep work,' focusing on only **one goal at a time**. Multitasking significantly reduces your productivity.
I once made a classic mistake: trying to handle group projects while preparing for midterms. Both deadlines were within the same week. When I finally sat down to open my textbook, my phone started buzzing. My group members were having a heated debate about the report's arguments in the chat, and I thought, 'Just replying once shouldn't matter.' That one reply turned into half an hour of arguing. By the time I put my phone down and returned to my books, my train of thought was long broken, my mind was blank, and I had to start reading all over again.

The result: I spent three hours and didn't even finish a single chapter. That sense of disconnect—where I was 'studying,' but my brain was still on my phone—was more exhausting than anything else.
Remember to set a 'no-distraction time' for yourself. Put your phone on 'Do Not Disturb,' disconnect from social media, and focus entirely on the single most important task. Leave trivial matters like replying to messages, organizing emails, or simple data collection for a specific, dedicated block of time.

However, be careful not to pack your calendar entirely with work. You need to leave room for **rest and unexpected situations**.

### Dynamic Adjustment

Time management is a dynamic collaboration; **plans must adapt to change**.
Take a few dozen minutes every week to review your progress and adjust your calendar for the coming week. If you find that the importance of a certain goal has decreased, or that your time allocation was too idealistic, don't hesitate to make changes. Re-evaluate your own work efficiency and set goals that are within your capabilities.

Effective time management isn't about doing more; it’s about freeing up time to 'experience' the beauty of university—to exercise, to read, to engage in deep conversations with like-minded friends—rather than wasting your life away on a library sofa.
