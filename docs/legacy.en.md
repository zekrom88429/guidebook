> This story is entirely fictional. Luo Youling is not based on any real person.

Luo Youling: the heaviest name in the annals of this university. Had it not been for her, this institution might have long since eroded into dust from sheer, stagnant tranquility. Every blade of grass on this campus has borne witness to her Oedipal crusade.

The legend began in her first year. The administration repurposed a patch of sunny lawn into classrooms, citing a shortage of space. Luo launched her first-ever protest, publishing a 2,000-word manifesto of blood and tears on social media, lamenting that "the soul has nowhere to rest." In the end, she garnered two likes—one from her own burner account, and the other from a friend who hit the button by mistake.

In her second year, she initiated a series of campaigns that shook the administration to its core:

* She vehemently protested the library’s use of "Ming" (Sim-Sun) font on signage, arguing that its lack of aesthetic design stifled students' creativity and freedom of thought. She demanded a complete switch to a serif typeface to uphold the university's humanistic integrity.
* She accused the school of setting the hot water temperature in the teaching building’s water dispensers at only 85°C, rather than the “golden temperature” of 92°C—which is ideal for brewing coffee—and strongly criticized the administration for effectively depriving students of their quality of life and refined tastes by lowering the hot water temperature.
* She attacked the university calendar for marking the "Exam Period" in red, asserting that such an intimidating color induced collective depression among students and demanding it be changed to a shade of light blue, symbolizing peace.

Her vision was a campus devoid of oppression, filled with aesthetics and justice. Her fighting spirit attracted as many as five "opponents" who joined her to form a massive organization called the "Campus Reform Concern Group," officially commencing a two-year Cold War with the administration office.

By her third year, Luo decided the group had reached "zero hour." That evening, four members gathered beside the administration building. Luo insisted on shouting at maximum volume, believing it to be a challenge to the systemic red line. When a security guard approached, she felt no shame; instead, a flicker of excitement flashed in her eyes. "Hello students, please keep your protest quiet. Don't disturb the dormitories," the guard gently reminded them.

She whispered to her members: "See? They’re panicking. They don't dare address our demands directly, so they send a pawn to engage in 'psychological warfare,' trying to break our resolve by citing 'noise disturbance.'" To her, that request from the guard was a sign that the highest levels of the administration were feeling unprecedented terror, even resorting to "dormitory public opinion" to suppress her. She viewed that minor warning as a historic victory for the group.

On the eve of graduation, she stood on the podium to recruit freshmen, her tone filled with a tragic hero’s arrogance. "I have led this university through its four darkest years," she told them. "Though the systems remain rigid, that only proves how formidable the enemy we face truly is—this 'utter lack of change' is the very proof of my existence."

Without her four years of struggle, this university would not be what it is today. Every institutional flaw is a "lesson" she has left for posterity. In her farewell speech, she framed these four years of futility as an "awakening movement." Even as she graduated to find the campus unchanged, she gazed at the gates that had never shifted, thinking to herself: "Thank goodness for me—it is precisely because of my presence that this mediocre school has preserved a flicker of resistance."
